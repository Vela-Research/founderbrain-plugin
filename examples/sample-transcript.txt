Tom: Thanks for making the time, Ana. I've read the deck, but I'd rather hear it from you. What are you building?
Ana Ruiz: Thanks for having me. We're building Harbourline, which is scheduling software for small freight terminals. If you picture a port or an inland rail yard, there are hundreds of trucks a day that need a slot to pick up or drop off a container, and at most of the terminals we talk to that is still done over the phone and in a shared spreadsheet. A dispatcher called Maria, at the first terminal we worked with, had three phones on her desk and a whiteboard behind her, and she told me on my first visit that her job was mostly saying no politely. So we give the terminal a booking page for truckers, we give the dispatcher one screen that shows the whole day, and we move slots around automatically when a ship is late. The result is that trucks stop queuing outside the gate, and the terminal gets through more boxes with the same crane time.
Tom: How did you end up working on this?
Ana Ruiz: I spent six years at a logistics company in Rotterdam, first as an analyst and then running operations for two of their inland terminals. The moment I remember is a Tuesday in February, three years ago. A ship came in eleven hours late, and by the afternoon we had a line of ninety trucks down the access road. The police called us because the queue was blocking a junction. I stood at the gate with a clipboard trying to decide who went first, and I realised that every one of those drivers had been given a time by someone on the phone, and none of those times meant anything any more. That afternoon cost us a penalty from the city and, I think, most of our goodwill with two haulage firms. I started sketching what a real booking system would look like that evening, and I have not really stopped since. My co-founder, Daniel, was the engineer who built the terminal's gate software, so he knew exactly where the data lived.
Tom: Where are you today, in numbers?
Ana Ruiz: We have seven terminals live, all in the Netherlands and Belgium. Together they handle about four thousand truck visits a day through Harbourline. Our annual recurring revenue is four hundred and twenty thousand euros, and it was one hundred and ten thousand a year ago, so it has grown a little under four times. Every terminal that started a paid pilot converted to a full contract, and none has left. The average gate wait at our terminals went from forty-one minutes to nine minutes in the first three months, and that number is the one terminal managers repeat to each other.
Tom: Who pays, and how much?
Ana Ruiz: The terminal pays. We charge a platform fee based on the number of gate lanes, and then a small fee per booking above a monthly allowance. A mid-sized terminal pays us about sixty thousand euros a year. Truckers use it for free, and that matters, because the truckers are the ones who spread the word. A haulage firm that uses us at one terminal starts asking every other terminal why they can't book the same way.
Tom: What does the competition look like?
Ana Ruiz: The big ports have systems built by the large port software vendors, and they are good at what they do, but they cost millions and take two years to install. The terminals we serve are small, two to eight gate lanes, and they cannot afford that. Most of them use spreadsheets, a phone line and sometimes a generic appointment tool that was built for hair salons. So in practice our competition is the spreadsheet. I would say there are two start-ups doing something similar in the United States, but neither of them works with the European customs systems, and that integration took Daniel most of last year.
Tom: Why hasn't one of the big vendors just built a smaller version?
Ana Ruiz: I think it is partly that the deal size is too small for their sales teams. A sixty thousand euro contract does not pay for a six-month sales cycle with solution architects flying out. We sell in about five weeks, mostly through one visit and a pilot. And partly it is that their products are built around the crane and the yard, not around the truck at the gate. We started from the gate, because that is where the pain was for me.
Tom: Tell me about a customer that almost didn't work out.
Ana Ruiz: The fourth terminal, in Antwerp, is the one I learned the most from. We went live on a Monday and by Wednesday the dispatcher had switched us off and gone back to the phones. I drove down the next morning and sat with her for a whole shift. It turned out that half of their trucks arrive without a booking because they come from a single large customer who has always just turned up. Our system treated those trucks as late, and it kept pushing them to the back of the queue, which made that customer furious. So we built what we now call standing slots, a fixed window each day for a named customer, and the dispatcher turned us back on the following week. That terminal is now our biggest account, and standing slots are used at five of the seven terminals. The lesson for me was that the spreadsheet had rules in it that nobody had written down, and our job is to find those rules before we switch anything off.
Tom: What would you do with the money?
Ana Ruiz: We are raising one and a half million euros. About half goes on engineering, mainly two more engineers so that Daniel is not the only person who understands the customs integration. A third goes on sales, which means one experienced salesperson who has sold into ports, and the rest is runway. With that we plan to go from seven terminals to twenty-five in eighteen months, and to open Germany, which has about ninety terminals of the size we serve.
Tom: Twenty-five sounds ambitious. What makes you confident?
Ana Ruiz: I think it is ambitious, yes. Honestly, I am not certain we will hit twenty-five, and I would rather tell you that now than in a year. What gives me some confidence is the pipeline. We have fourteen terminals that have asked for a pilot, and nine of them came to us through haulage firms rather than through our own outreach. The part I am less sure about is Germany. The customs system there is different, and we have not yet built that integration, so the German number depends on how quickly Daniel and a new engineer can do it. If Germany is slow, I think we still get to eighteen or twenty terminals in the Benelux and France.
Tom: What keeps you up at night?
Ana Ruiz: Two things. The first is that we are still very dependent on Daniel. He built the integrations, and if he were ill for a month we would have a real problem, which is why the first hire is an engineer. The second is that a terminal operator group buys three or four of our customers and moves them onto a big vendor system. That has not happened yet, and our customers tell us they would fight it, but it is a risk I think about.
Tom: How do you and Daniel split the work?
Ana Ruiz: I run everything that faces the customer, which means sales, onboarding and support, and I also do the finances. Daniel runs the product and engineering. We make hiring decisions together, and we have one rule, which is that either of us can veto a hire. We have used that twice. We have known each other for eight years, and we have had one real disagreement, about whether to build standing slots or a general rules engine. He wanted the rules engine. We built standing slots first because the Antwerp dispatcher needed it that week, and I think he would now say that was right, though he still wants the rules engine.
Tom: What do you look for in an investor?
Ana Ruiz: Someone who has seen a vertical software company grow from a handful of customers to a few hundred, because the mistakes at that stage are ones I have not made yet. I also want someone who will be honest with me when the numbers are not good. Our previous angel round was mostly friends from the logistics industry, and they are wonderful about introductions but less useful when I need someone to tell me that a plan does not add up.
Tom: If this works, what does Harbourline look like in five years?
Ana Ruiz: I would like every small and mid-sized terminal in Europe to book its trucks through us, which is somewhere around six hundred terminals. At that point we know more about truck flows between ports and inland yards than anyone else, and we can start offering the haulage firms something too, such as planning their routes around real gate times instead of guesses. But I want to be careful here, because that second product is an idea, not a plan. What I know works today is the booking system, and that is what the money is for.
Tom: Last question. Why you?
Ana Ruiz: Because I have stood at that gate with the clipboard. When I visit a terminal, I know what the dispatcher's day looks like, and they can tell within ten minutes that I have done their job. That gets us into the room. Daniel knows the systems behind the gate better than anyone I have met, and that is why we can install in weeks rather than years. And I think the Antwerp story is the best answer I can give you. When something breaks, we go and sit next to the person it broke for, and we fix it that week.
